Part 1

Deterministic Optimization Foundations

Before introducing stochasticity, we establish the classical mechanics of descending a potential landscape $L(\theta)$.

Gradient Descent & Gradient Flow

Intuition: Gradient descent takes discrete steps along the steepest local slope. As step size vanishes, this sequence traces the gradient flow, a continuous path moving orthogonally to contour lines.
Gradient Descent: The parameter vector is updated iteratively via: $$ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - \eta \nabla L(\theta_n) $$ If the objective $L$ is $M$-smooth (i.e., its gradient is $M$-Lipschitz continuous, $\|\nabla L(\theta) - \nabla L(\theta')\| \leqslant M\|\theta - \theta'\|$), a fixed learning rate $\eta \leqslant 1/M$ ensures monotonic descent and achieves a convergence rate of $L(\theta_n) - L(\theta^*) = \mathcal{O}(1/n)$ (Nesterov, 2004, "Introductory Lectures on Convex Optimization", Theorem 2.1.14).
Gradient Flow: Taking the continuous-time limit $\eta \to 0$ transforms the difference equation into an ordinary differential equation (ODE): $$ \dot{\overline{\theta}}_t = -\nabla L(\overline{\theta}_t) $$ This describes the macroscopic mean trajectory around which stochastic variations fluctuate.
Landscape: Himmelblau's function $L(x,y) = (x^2+y-11)^2 + (x+y^2-7)^2$
Gradient: $$ \nabla L(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} 4x(x^2+y-11)+2(x+y^2-7) \\ 2(x^2+y-11)+4y(x+y^2-7) \end{bmatrix} $$
3D view: The discrete steps of Gradient Descent (red) compared to the theoretical continuous flow (white) over the loss surface.
2D view: Gradient flow moves orthogonally to the contours into the local basin of attraction.

Newton's Method & High-Dimensional Bottlenecks

Intuition: Gradient descent uses a linear approximation; Newton's method uses a quadratic approximation, jumping to its minimum in one step.
Newton Update: The step requires the inverse of the Hessian matrix $\nabla^2 L(\theta_n)$: $$ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - [\nabla^2 L(\theta_n)]^{-1} \nabla L(\theta_n) $$ Kantorovich Theorem: Newton's method yields local quadratic convergence. If the Hessian is $M_2$-Lipschitz continuous and the objective is $\mu$-strongly convex in a neighborhood of the optimum $\theta^*$, the iterates satisfy: $$ \|\theta_{n+1} - \theta^*\| \leqslant C \|\theta_n - \theta^*\|^2 $$ where the constant $C = \frac{M_2}{2\mu}$ is computed as the supremum of the Hessian's Lipschitz constant scaled by the inverse of the minimal curvature. This requires an initialization $\theta_0 \in \mathcal{N}$ within the basin of attraction such that $C\|\theta_0 - \theta^*\| < 1$ (Kantorovich, 1948, "Functional analysis and applied mathematics").
The Bottleneck: In modern machine learning models with millions of parameters, constructing and inverting the $d \times d$ Hessian matrix imposes an $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ computational cost per step, necessitating the reliance on first-order approximations.

Line Search & Bisection Methods

Intuition: Evaluating full $d$-dimensional gradients is computationally expensive. Freezing the descent direction reduces optimization to a 1D curve. 1D root-finding (like bisection) then rapidly locates the minimal step size.
Exact Line Search: Given a descent direction $p_n$ (e.g., $p_n = -\nabla L(\theta_n)$), we seek the step size $\eta$ that minimizes the loss along the ray: $$ \min_{\eta > 0} \phi(\eta) := L(\theta_n + \eta p_n) $$ Bisection Method: For strictly convex loss functions, the directional derivative $\phi'(\eta) = \nabla L(\theta_n + \eta p_n)^\top p_n$ is increasing. Because $\phi'(0) < 0$, the optimal step size occurs where $\phi'(\eta) = 0$. This root can be found logarithmically fast via binary search (bisection) along the $\eta$ axis, bypassing the need for full multivariate evaluations at each test point (Nocedal & Wright, 2006, "Numerical Optimization", Section 3.1). In practice, weak Wolfe conditions often replace exact bisection to ensure sufficient decrease with minimal computational overhead.
Part 2

Statistical Learning, Neural Networks & Generalization

Optimization in deep learning differs fundamentally from pure optimization because minimizing the training loss is merely an indirect means of achieving good predictive performance on unseen data.

Function Classes & Empirical Risk Minimization

Intuition: Learning is optimization: we select a parameterized function family and tune its parameters to minimize prediction errors on training data.
Neural Networks as Function Classes: A neural network defines a hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$ through nested linear transformations and non-linear activation functions. For an input $x$, a feedforward network of depth $L$ is mathematically expressed as: $$ h_\theta(x) = W_L \sigma_{L-1}(W_{L-1} \dots \sigma_1(W_1 x + b_1) \dots + b_{L-1}) + b_L $$ where the parameters $\theta$ encompass all weight matrices $W_k$ and bias vectors $b_k$. The functions $\sigma_k(\cdot)$ are component-wise non-linear activations (e.g., ReLU $\max(0, z)$, Sigmoid, or Tanh), allowing the network to approximate complex non-linear functions.
Transformers & Architecture Inductive Biases: Modern architectures like Transformers deviate from dense, fully connected layers to enforce specific inductive biases. Transformers utilize Self-Attention mechanisms to dynamically weight the spatial or sequential closeness of input tokens, computing transformations like: $$ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V $$ This structural sparsity prevents the network from having to learn sequential relevance purely from data.
The Optimization Objective: Given a loss function $\ell$ measuring prediction error, our ultimate goal is to find the parameter $\theta^*$ that minimizes the expected risk over the true, unknown data-generating distribution $\mathcal{D}$: $$ \theta^* = \arg\min_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d} \mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim \mathcal{D}} [\ell(h_\theta(X), Y)] $$ Because $\mathcal{D}$ is inaccessible, we replace the expected risk with Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) calculated over a finite, observable training set $\mathcal{S} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$: $$ \hat{\theta} = \arg\min_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ell(h_\theta(x_i), y_i) $$
A representation of Empirical Risk Minimization restricting the global search strictly to the parameterized hypothesis class (the boundary is defined by the unit circle mapped into space).

The Generalization Problem & Flat Minima

Intuition: Reaching the absolute empirical minimum often causes overfitting, landing in sharp traps. We prefer flat minima, which tolerate slight landscape shifts between training and test data.
The Generalization Gap: The difference between the empirical risk $L(\theta)$ and the true population risk $J(\theta)$ is the generalization error. Reaching the exact global minimizer of $L(\theta)$ often causes severe overfitting. Algorithms whose inherent stochastic dynamics drive the parameters away from sharp empirical minima and into flat minima are preferred (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997, "Flat minima").
The true Population Risk (blue) is smooth, but we only have access to the Empirical Risk (solid multi-color), which contains spurious sharp local minima generated by overfitting to finite training data. The Empirical Minimizer sits in a sharp trap, causing high generalization error on the true landscape.
Part 3

Geometries & Stochastic Gradient Descent

Stochastic convergence guarantees rely heavily on the geometric assumptions of the loss landscape $\ell(\theta)$ and precise control of the noise sequence.

Loss Landscape Geometries

Strong Convexity: A function $\ell(\theta)$ is strongly convex if there exists $\mu > 0$ such that the Hessian satisfies $\nabla^2 \ell(\theta) \succeq \mu I$. This ensures a unique minimizer $\theta^*$ and convergence is evaluated by the distance to the optimum $\mathbb{E}[\|\theta_n - \theta^*\|^2]$.
Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) Condition: Relaxing strict convexity, the PL condition states that the gradient norm bounds the suboptimality of the objective function (Polyak, 1963, "Gradient methods for the minimisation of functionals"): $$ \frac{1}{2}\|\nabla \ell(\theta)\|^2 \geqslant \mu (\ell(\theta) - \ell(\theta^*)) $$ This permits non-convexity, allowing for continuous loci of global minima (e.g., flat valleys). If $\ell(\theta)$ is also $M$-smooth, gradient descent utilizing a fixed step size $\eta = 1/M$ still achieves a linear convergence rate: $$ \ell(\theta_n) - \ell(\theta^*) \leqslant \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{M}\right)^n (\ell(\theta_0) - \ell(\theta^*)) $$
Strong Convexity ($f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$) ensures a steep bowl with a single, easily locatable point of global minimum.
The PL Condition ($f(x,y) = (y - x^2)^2$) accommodates flat, non-linear valleys where a continuous curve of points all represent valid global minima.

Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) & Mini-Batches

Intuition: Exact gradients require evaluating the full dataset. SGD uses noisy gradients from mini-batches. The learning rate must decay to dampen this sampling variance.
Mini-Batch Estimation: In deep learning, calculating the exact empirical gradient requires evaluating every point: $$ \nabla L(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \nabla \ell(h_\theta(x_i), y_i) $$ This is an expensive $\mathcal{O}(N)$ operation. We replace the true gradient with an unbiased estimator calculated over a small random subset $\mathcal{B} \subset \mathcal{S}$ of size $B$ (a mini-batch). This dramatically reduces computational cost per step while ensuring the expectation of the stochastic gradient matches the full-batch gradient: $\mathbb{E}[g_n \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = \nabla L(\theta_n)$.
Update Rule: $$ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - \eta_n g_n $$ Convergence Conditions: Almost sure convergence to a local minimum demands that the stochastic gradient possesses bounded variance, $\mathbb{E}[\|g_n - \nabla L(\theta_n)\|^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \leqslant \sigma^2$, and that the learning rates satisfy the Robbins-Monro conditions (Robbins & Monro, 1951, "A stochastic approximation method"): $$ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \eta_n = \infty \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{n=1}^\infty \eta_n^2 < \infty $$
Landscape (4 Modes): $$ U(x) = -3\exp(-10(x-2)^2) - 3\exp(-10(x+2)^2) - 2.5\exp(-0.2(x-6)^2) - 2.5\exp(-0.2(x+6)^2) + 0.05x^2 $$
Gradient: $$ \nabla U(x) = 60(x-2)e^{-10(x-2)^2} + 60(x+2)e^{-10(x+2)^2} + (x-6)e^{-0.2(x-6)^2} + (x+6)e^{-0.2(x+6)^2} + 0.1x $$
The SGD path (red) escapes the suboptimal narrow minima at $\pm 2$ and wanders into the wider robust minima at $\pm 6$ due to the injected mini-batch variance. Notice how doubling the noise or learning rate increases volatility.

SGD with Momentum

Intuition: SGD oscillates in steep ravines and stalls on plateaus. Momentum accumulates past gradients, accelerating consistent descent and canceling erratic oscillations.
Momentum Update Rule: We introduce a velocity vector $v_n$ and a decay parameter $\beta \in [0, 1)$ (typically $0.9$): $$ v_{n} = \beta v_{n-1} + g_n $$ $$ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - \eta v_n $$ This physically simulates a heavy particle rolling down the loss landscape. The momentum term $\beta v_{n-1}$ forces the trajectory to maintain its previous direction, actively suppressing the high-variance stochastic noise of mini-batch sampling and accelerating convergence through regions with low curvature (Polyak, 1964, "Some methods of speeding up the convergence of iteration methods").

AdaGrad & First-Order Variations

AdaGrad Update Rule: Let $G_{n,i} = \sum_{t=1}^n g_{t,i}^2$ be the accumulated squared gradient for coordinate $i$. The update scales coordinates inversely to their historical gradients to normalize ill-conditioned landscape curvatures (Duchi, Hazan, & Singer, 2011, "Adaptive Subgradient Methods for Online Learning and Stochastic Optimization"): $$ \theta_{n+1, i} = \theta_{n, i} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{G_{n, i} + \epsilon}} g_{n, i} $$

Adam & Structural Divergence

Intuition: Adam normalizes gradient magnitudes using moving averages; it is not a second-order approximation.
Adam Update Rule: With decay parameters $\beta_1, \beta_2 \in [0,1)$ and bias corrections $\hat{m}_n, \hat{v}_n$ (Kingma & Ba, 2014, "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization"): $$ m_n = \beta_1 m_{n-1} + (1 - \beta_1) g_n \quad \implies \quad \hat{m}_n = m_n / (1 - \beta_1^n) $$ $$ v_n = \beta_2 v_{n-1} + (1 - \beta_2) (g_n \odot g_n) \quad \implies \quad \hat{v}_n = v_n / (1 - \beta_2^n) $$ $$ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_n} + \epsilon} \odot \hat{m}_n $$
Divergence & Vector Field Distortion: The exponential decay mechanism causes Adam to diverge or converge to suboptimal points in specific convex settings (Reddi, Kale, & Kumar, 2018, "On the Convergence of Adam and Beyond"). Furthermore, Adam does not universally converge to critical points ($\nabla \ell(\theta) = 0$). It instead converges to the roots of a distinct, algorithm-induced "Adam vector field" (Dereich & Jentzen, 2024, "Convergence rates for the Adam optimizer").
Elliptical Landscape with Local Minima: $$ U(x,y) = 0.02(x^2 + y^2) - c_1 \exp(-0.2(x-3)^2 - 0.8(y-3)^2) - c_2 \exp(-0.8(x+3)^2 - 0.2(y+3)^2) $$
Full-batch deterministic trajectories across asymmetric local minima. Notice how Adam's spatial rescaling allows it to dive directly into deep basins, whereas Vanilla Gradient Descent struggles with flat regions and Momentum frequently overshoots the wells.
Stochastic Variants (Mini-Batch Simulation)
Stochastic optimization trajectories incorporating additive exogenous noise to simulate mini-batch estimation variance. Adam and Momentum trajectories maintain their structural navigation advantages over standard SGD despite the severe noise injections.

Gradient Clipping & Heavy-Tailed Noise

Intuition: While the variance from mini-batch sampling aids in escaping sharp minima, neural networks often exhibit heavy-tailed (infinite-variance) gradients. Large, abrupt jumps push the algorithm out of sharp valleys, but to prevent the algorithm from destabilizing entirely, we apply a hard speed limit (clipping).
Gradient Clipping: A standard technique to prevent explosive divergence is to truncate the gradient vector if its norm exceeds a threshold $c > 0$: $$ \hat{g}_n = g_n \min\left(1, \frac{c}{\|g_n\|}\right) $$ Escaping Sharp Minima: Heavy-tailed gradient noise, when combined with clipping, creates a state-dependent noise structure (Wang, Oh, & Rhee, 2021, "Eliminating sharp minima from SGD with truncated heavy-tailed noise"). This truncation mechanism actively drives the SGD trajectory away from sharp minima and funnels it into wider, flatter minima, improving out-of-sample generalization.

Median of Means (MoM) Estimator

Intuition: A challenge of gradient clipping is that the optimal clip threshold $c$ is difficult to determine. To achieve the same heavy-tail robustness without arbitrary parameter tuning, we can partition the mini-batch data and take the spatial median of the sub-gradients rather than the global mean.
The Escape Time Problem: The optimal clip size $c$ depends entirely on the minimal number of steps of size $c$ required to escape a specific basin of attraction. Computing this requires global topographical knowledge of the loss landscape, rendering systematic tuning impractical.
Tuning-Free Robustness: In ongoing work by Mario Molina and the author, we replace clipped mean estimation with a Median-of-Means (MoM) approach. By randomly partitioning the mini-batch into subgroups, computing the mean gradient of each subgroup, and taking the spatial median of those means, the estimator achieves asymptotic robustness to heavy-tailed noise. This neutralizes gradient explosions and preserves flat minima preferences without requiring the calculation of $c$.

The Modern Machine Learning Pipeline

1. Expected Risk

The true objective: minimize the population error over the unknown data distribution $\mathcal{D}$.

2. Empirical Risk

Substitute the expected risk with the average error evaluated on a finite training dataset $\mathcal{S}$.

3. Stochastic Optimizer

Apply algorithms like SGD or Adam. The injected noise prevents collapsing into narrow empirical traps.

4. Flat Minima

Settle near broad, flat valleys. These regions are structurally robust to discrepancies between empirical and true risk.

Pedagogical Note: Since this is empirical risk minimization, nailing down the exact global minimum of the training data is not only unimportant, it is actively detrimental (overfitting). The stochasticity of the final position, a natural by-product of using a stochastic optimizer, is highly desirable. Unlike classical approximation schemes (such as Robbins-Monro) where learning schedules strictly decay to zero, modern practices keep learning rates bounded away from zero. We want the learning rate to be just small enough that the algorithm gravitates toward local minima, yet large enough that it perpetually fluctuates and bounces out of sharp, unreliable crevices.

Langevin Dynamics & Hamiltonian Principles

Treating the loss function as a potential energy landscape $U(\theta) = L(\theta)$ maps discrete optimization updates to continuous-time physical systems. We often have functions we want to sample from but whose shape is irregular. For instance, the posteriors in certain Bayesian models. To this end, we set up Markov processes with these target distributions as invariant measures. One highly popular framework for this is via Overdamped Langevin Dynamics.

Overdamped Langevin Dynamics (OLD)

Intuition: OLD simulates a massless particle in a viscous medium, driven by the potential gradient and Gaussian thermal noise.
Formal SDE: Let $U(\theta)$ be a regular potential function. Standard OLD is governed by: $$ \mathrm{d}\theta_t = -\nabla U(\theta_t)\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{2T}\mathrm{d}W_t $$ where $T>0$ is the temperature and $W_t$ is standard Brownian motion. Its stationary distribution is the canonical Gibbs measure $\pi(\theta) \propto \exp(-U(\theta)/T)$.

Underdamped Langevin & Hamiltonian Mechanics

Intuition: OLD is a purely diffusive random walk, making it slow in high dimensions. ULD adds physical momentum, allowing the particle to coast along contours and cross shallow barriers. This directed exploration underpins Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC).
Formal SDE: Let $\theta$ represent the position coordinates and $p$ represent the auxiliary momentum variables. For a mass matrix $M$, the Hamiltonian (total energy) is $H(\theta, p) = U(\theta) + \frac{1}{2} p^\top M^{-1} p$. The ULD acts as a stochastic perturbation of the pure Hamiltonian flow: $$ \mathrm{d}\theta_t = \nabla_p H(\theta_t, p_t)\mathrm{d}t = M^{-1}p_t \mathrm{d}t $$ $$ \mathrm{d}p_t = -\nabla_\theta H(\theta_t, p_t)\mathrm{d}t - \gamma M^{-1}p_t \mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{2\gamma T}\mathrm{d}W_t $$ where $\gamma > 0$ is the friction coefficient.
Connection to Optimization: The Stochastic Gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (SGHMC) algorithm discretizes this system, yielding an update structurally identical to SGD with momentum, establishing a mapping between the friction coefficient $\gamma$ and the optimization momentum parameter $\beta$.
Select Potential Landscape:
OLD (dark red) diffuses slowly into the nearest minimum basin. ULD (orange, integrated via Leapfrog) utilizes Hamiltonian momentum to coast and spiral dynamically, accelerating space exploration and crossing flat barriers.

Lévy-Driven Langevin (L-OLD) for Heavy Tails

Intuition: For heavy-tailed targets, continuous Brownian motion is replaced by a jump process. Abrupt jumps enable escapes from local traps.
Formal SDE: The Lévy-driven Overdamped Langevin equation replaces $W_t$ with a purely discontinuous Lévy process $L_t$ (e.g., an $\alpha$-stable process for $\alpha \in (0, 2)$): $$ \mathrm{d}\theta_t = -\nabla U(\theta_t) \mathrm{d}t + \mathrm{d}L_t $$ The standard local differential generator $\mathcal{A}$ transitions into a non-local integro-differential operator: $$ \mathcal{A}f(\theta) = -\nabla U(\theta) \cdot \nabla f(\theta) + \int_{\mathbb{R}^d \setminus \{0\}} \left(f(\theta + x) - f(\theta) - \nabla f(\theta) \cdot x \mathbb{I}_{\|x\| \leqslant 1}\right) \nu(\mathrm{d}x) $$ where $\nu$ is the jump measure of $L_t$.
Double Well Potential: $U(x) = x^4 - 4x^2 + 0.5x$
Gradient: $\nabla U(x) = 4x^3 - 8x + 0.5$
The Brownian path (blue) becomes trapped in local basins, while the heavy-tailed Lévy path (orange) executes large jumps to traverse between modes.

Diffeomorphic MCMC for Heavy Tails

Intuition: While OLD and ULD typically converge exponentially fast if $U$ grows polynomially (light tails), they converge extremely slowly if $U$ grows logarithmically (heavy tails). By applying an invertible transformation (a diffeomorphism), we map a heavy-tailed target distribution into a light-tailed one.
Transformation Framework: Let the original heavy-tailed target measure be $\pi(\theta) \propto \exp(-U(\theta))$. We introduce a diffeomorphism $h: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ such that $\theta = h(\eta)$. The transformed target measure is $\tilde{\pi}(\eta) \propto \exp(-\tilde{U}(\eta))$, where the new potential includes the log-determinant of the Jacobian: $$ \tilde{U}(\eta) = U(h(\eta)) - \log |\det \nabla h(\eta)| $$
Transformation Recipe: If $U(\theta)$ grows logarithmically, we require a map $h(\eta)$ that compresses the space exponentially fast. An isotropic recipe defines $h(\eta) = \eta \cdot \exp(\|\eta\|^2)$. The heavy tails of $\pi(\theta)$ are mapped to Gaussian-like tails in $\tilde{\pi}(\eta)$. Standard OLD applied to $\tilde{U}(\eta)$ achieves geometric ergodicity, and samples are mapped back via $h(\eta)$ (Brešar & Mijatović, 2026, "Diffeomorphic Markov Chain Monte Carlo").
Homeomorphism: Mapping latent grid $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^2$ to target space $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^2$
Transformation: $h(\eta) = \eta \exp(c \|\eta\|^2)$
Level sets of the isotropic potential (blue circles) map onto exponentially expanded level sets in the target space (red circles), visualizing how the diffeomorphism flattens heavy tails.

The MCMC Sampling Challenge

1. The Target Density $\pi(\theta)$

We start with a target density we want to sample from, such as a complex Bayesian posterior. We design an MCMC process with this density as its stationary law, hoping it will converge.

2. Overdamped Langevin Dynamics (OLD)

We construct a diffusion process. The Issue: If the landscape is multimodal, OLD easily gets trapped in local potential minima. The process must be periodically restarted.

3a. Heavy-Tail Jumps (L-OLD)

Introduce jumps to escape local modes. The Issue: The invariant law changes and finding the exact new invariant law analytically is extremely difficult.

3b. Hamiltonian Dynamics (ULD)

Introduce physical momentum to coast through barriers with repelling-attracting behaviors. The Issue: Can still struggle with excessively heavy tails.

3c. Diffeomorphic MCMC

Apply a general diffeomorphic transformation. Compress heavy tails down to light-tailed data to drastically accelerate mixing without losing exactness. The Issue: Does nothing to address the multimodal problem.

Stationary Densities & Reversibility

To prove that the canonical Gibbs measure serves as the stationary density for Langevin systems, we analyze the structure of their infinitesimal generators using resolvents and formal self-adjointness criteria.

Stationarity and the Infinitesimal Generator

Intuition: A distribution $\pi$ is stationary if the expected value of any observable function remains constant over time. If the generator annihilates the measure, the flow of probability is balanced.
Stationarity Theorem: A probability measure $\pi$ is an invariant measure for a Markov process with generator $\mathcal{A}$ if and only if for all bounded, smooth functions $f \in C^\infty_b$: $$ \int \mathcal{A} f(x) \pi(x) \mathrm{d}x = 0 \quad \text{i.e.,} \quad \pi \mathcal{A} = 0 $$
Proof via Resolvents: To establish that $\pi \mathcal{A} = 0 \implies \pi P_t = \pi$, we utilize the resolvent operator $R_\lambda f = \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t} P_t f \mathrm{d}t$ for $\lambda > 0$. The resolvent equation states that $\lambda R_\lambda f - f = \mathcal{A} R_\lambda f$. Integrating both sides with respect to $\pi$ yields: $$ \int (\lambda R_\lambda f - f) \mathrm{d}\pi = \int \mathcal{A} R_\lambda f \mathrm{d}\pi $$ Because $\pi \mathcal{A} = 0$, the right side vanishes. Consequently, $\lambda \int R_\lambda f \mathrm{d}\pi = \int f \mathrm{d}\pi$. By the uniqueness of the Laplace transform, this guarantees that $\int P_t f \mathrm{d}\pi = \int f \mathrm{d}\pi$ for all $t \geqslant 0$, proving $\pi$ is stationary.

Reversibility and Self-Adjointness

Intuition: A stochastic process is reversible if it looks statistically identical when played backwards in time. This occurs when there is no net flow of probability between any two states, known as detailed balance.
Theorem: A process is $\pi$-reversible if and only if its generator $\mathcal{A}$ is self-adjoint in $L^2(\pi)$, meaning $\langle f, \mathcal{A} g \rangle_\pi = \langle \mathcal{A} f, g \rangle_\pi$. All such reversible processes have the invariant density $\pi$.
Proof: Detailed balance equates to the symmetry of the transition semigroup: $\langle f, P_t g \rangle_\pi = \langle P_t f, g \rangle_\pi$. Differentiating this relation at $t=0$ yields the self-adjointness of the generator: $\langle f, \mathcal{A} g \rangle_\pi = \langle \mathcal{A} f, g \rangle_\pi$. To prove that reversibility implies stationarity, substitute $f=1$. Because the generator satisfies $\mathcal{A}(1) = 0$, self-adjointness enforces: $$ \langle 1, \mathcal{A} g \rangle_\pi = \langle \mathcal{A}(1), g \rangle_\pi = 0 $$ Thus $\int \mathcal{A}g(x)\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x = 0$, satisfying the Stationarity Theorem.

Verifying Reversibility in OLD and ULD

OLD is Reversible: The OLD generator is $\mathcal{A}_{OLD} f = -\nabla U \cdot \nabla f + T \Delta f$. We check symmetry in $L^2(\pi)$ where $\pi \propto \exp(-U/T)$: $$ \langle g, \mathcal{A}_{OLD} f \rangle_\pi = \int g (-\nabla U \cdot \nabla f + T \Delta f) e^{-U/T} \mathrm{d}x $$ Applying integration by parts to the Laplacian term gives: $$ T \int g \Delta f e^{-U/T} \mathrm{d}x = -T \int \nabla g \cdot \nabla f e^{-U/T} \mathrm{d}x - T \int g \nabla f \cdot \nabla (e^{-U/T}) \mathrm{d}x $$ Because $T\nabla (e^{-U/T}) = -\nabla U e^{-U/T}$, the last term evaluates to $\int g \nabla f \cdot \nabla U e^{-U/T} \mathrm{d}x$, exactly canceling the drift component. We are left with: $$ \langle g, \mathcal{A}_{OLD} f \rangle_\pi = -T \int \nabla f \cdot \nabla g e^{-U/T} \mathrm{d}x $$ This form is perfectly symmetric in $f$ and $g$, proving self-adjointness and thus reversibility.
ULD Invariant Density: The ULD generator is $\mathcal{A}_{ULD} = \mathcal{A}_{Ham} + \mathcal{A}_{FD}$, where $\mathcal{A}_{Ham} f = \nabla_p H \cdot \nabla_\theta f - \nabla_\theta H \cdot \nabla_p f$ and $\mathcal{A}_{FD} f = \gamma \left( T \Delta_p f - \nabla_p H \cdot \nabla_p f \right)$.

To prove that $\pi(\theta, p) \propto \exp(-H(\theta,p)/T)$ is invariant, we apply the Fokker-Planck adjoint operator $\mathcal{A}^*_{ULD}$ to $\pi$. The system decomposes into two independent annihilations:
  • Hamiltonian Flow: $\mathcal{A}^*_{Ham} \pi = -\nabla_\theta \cdot (\pi \nabla_p H) + \nabla_p \cdot (\pi \nabla_\theta H)$. Because $\nabla_\theta \pi = -\frac{1}{T} \pi \nabla_\theta H$ and $\nabla_p \pi = -\frac{1}{T} \pi \nabla_p H$, expanding these terms yields $\frac{1}{T}\pi \nabla_\theta H \cdot \nabla_p H - \frac{1}{T}\pi \nabla_p H \cdot \nabla_\theta H = 0$. By Liouville's theorem, this evaluates identically to $0$.
  • Fluctuation-Dissipation: $\mathcal{A}^*_{FD} \pi = \gamma \nabla_p \cdot \left( \pi \nabla_p H + T \nabla_p \pi \right)$. Direct substitution of $\pi$ shows that $T \nabla_p \pi = -\pi \nabla_p H$, causing the term inside the divergence to vanish identically.
Because $\mathcal{A}^*_{ULD} \pi = 0$, the target measure is invariant.
Part 6

Methodologies of Proof

Theoretical guarantees for stochastic optimization and Langevin sampling hinge on mathematical frameworks. We distinguish between conditions that guarantee exact almost sure convergence (via supermartingales) and those establishing invariant measures via generalized Lyapunov drift inequalities.

Robbins-Monro Stochastic Approximation

Formal Definition: The goal is to find the root $\theta^*$ of an equation $h(\theta) = 0$. When $h(\theta)$ can only be observed via a noisy estimator $H(\theta_n, \xi_{n+1})$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}[H(\theta_n, \xi_{n+1}) \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = h(\theta_n)$, the Robbins-Monro recursion is: $$ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - \eta_n H(\theta_n, \xi_{n+1}) $$ where step sizes must satisfy the classical infinite reach and bounded variance conditions (Robbins & Monro, 1951, "A stochastic approximation method"): $$ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \eta_n = \infty \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{n=1}^\infty \eta_n^2 < \infty $$

Robbins-Siegmund Almost Supermartingale Theorem

Intuition: Non-negative supermartingales eventually converge to a finite limit. Robbins-Siegmund extends this: if upward energy injections are globally summable, the system cools down and converges almost surely.
Formal Property: For a filtration $\mathcal{F}_n$ and non-negative $\mathcal{F}_n$-measurable variables $V_n, a_n, b_n, c_n$, the conditional drift inequality is (Robbins & Siegmund, 1971, "A convergence theorem for non negative almost supermartingales and some applications"): $$ \mathbb{E}[V_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \leqslant (1 + a_n)V_n - b_n + c_n $$ If $\sum a_n < \infty$ and $\sum c_n < \infty$ almost surely, then $V_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} V_\infty < \infty$ and $\sum b_n < \infty$ almost surely.
Proof Outline: We use a localization argument. Define the stopping time: $$ \tau_M = \inf\left\{n \geqslant 0 : V_0 > M \text{ or } \sum_{k=0}^n a_k > M \text{ or } \sum_{k=0}^n c_k > M\right\} $$ Because $\tau_M$ does not depend on the forward evolution of $V_n$, the hypotheses $\sum a_n < \infty$ and $\sum c_n < \infty$ ensure that $\mathbb{P}(\cup_M \{\tau_M = \infty\}) = 1$. We stop the primary process at $\tau_M$, but crucially, we force the step size variables to strictly vanish after the stopping time using an indicator function: $$ \tilde{V}_n = V_{n \wedge \tau_M}, \quad \tilde{a}_n = a_n \mathbb{I}_{\{n < \tau_M\}}, \quad \tilde{b}_n = b_n \mathbb{I}_{\{n < \tau_M\}}, \quad \tilde{c}_n = c_n \mathbb{I}_{\{n < \tau_M\}} $$ Because the step variables are zeroed out after $\tau_M$, the stopped process trivially preserves the drift inequality identically for all $n$: $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{V}_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] \leqslant (1 + \tilde{a}_n)\tilde{V}_n - \tilde{b}_n + \tilde{c}_n$. We construct the integrating factor $\tilde A_n = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+\tilde{a}_k) \leqslant e^M$ and define the transformed process: $$ W_n = \frac{\tilde{V}_n}{\tilde A_n} + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac{\tilde{b}_k}{\tilde A_{k+1}} + \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{k=n}^\infty \frac{\tilde{c}_k}{\tilde A_{k+1}} \mathrel{\Big|} \mathcal{F}_n \right]. $$ Taking the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[W_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n]$ shows that $W_n$ is a strict non-negative supermartingale. By Doob's martingale convergence theorem, $W_n$ converges to a finite limit almost surely.

To prove the summability of $b_n$, take the unconditional expectation of the stopped drift inequality: $$ \mathbb{E}[\tilde{V}_{n+1}] \leqslant \mathbb{E}[\tilde{V}_n] + \mathbb{E}[\tilde{a}_n \tilde{V}_n] - \mathbb{E}[\tilde{b}_n] + \mathbb{E}[\tilde{c}_n] $$ Summing this from $n=0$ to $N$ telescopes the $V$ terms, yielding: $$ \sum_{n=0}^N \mathbb{E}[\tilde{b}_n] \leqslant \mathbb{E}[V_0] - \mathbb{E}[\tilde{V}_{N+1}] + \sum_{n=0}^N \left(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{a}_n \tilde{V}_n] + \mathbb{E}[\tilde{c}_n]\right) $$ Because $\tilde{V}_n$ converges to a finite random variable (ensuring $\sup_n \mathbb{E}[\tilde{a}_n \tilde{V}_n] < \infty$ since $\sum \tilde{a}_n \leqslant M$) and $\sum \tilde{c}_n \leqslant M$, the right-hand side is uniformly bounded above for all $N$. Taking the limit as $N \to \infty$ proves that $\mathbb{E}[\sum_{n=0}^\infty \tilde{b}_n] < \infty$, which guarantees that $\sum_{n=0}^\infty \tilde{b}_n < \infty$ almost surely. Because the stopping time $\tau_M$ eventually goes to infinity, the unstopped process $V_n$ converges to a finite limit $V_\infty$ and $\sum b_n < \infty$ almost surely.

Lyapunov Drift Upper Bounds (Discrete & Continuous)

Intuition: For continuous-time Markov chains or constant step-size optimization, the noise never completely vanishes. The process reaches a stationary distribution by balancing a strong inward drift from the boundaries against thorough mixing in the center. The landscape's geometry dictates this convergence speed.
Small and Petite Sets: A set $C \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})$ is a small set if there exists a time $t > 0$, a constant $\epsilon > 0$, and a probability measure $\nu$ such that the transition kernel satisfies $P_t(x, B) \geqslant \epsilon \nu(B)$ for all $x \in C$ and measurable sets $B$ (Meyn & Tweedie, 1993, "Markov Chains and Stochastic Stability").
Connection to the Martingale Problem: The drift condition is linked to the martingale problem for the generator $\mathcal{A}$. If $\mathcal{A}V(\theta) \leqslant -\phi(V(\theta)) + b \mathbb{I}_C(\theta)$, this inequality forces the system's energy $V(X_t)$ to act as a supermartingale (actively dissipating energy) whenever the process wanders outside the central set $C$.
Continuous-Time Drift Theorem: For continuous-time processes, the differential generator condition is identically structured (Douc, Fort, & Guillin, 2009, "Subgeometric rates of convergence of f-ergodic strong Markov processes"): $$ \mathcal{A}V(\theta) \leqslant -\phi(V(\theta)) + b \mathbb{I}_C(\theta) $$ Yielding the continuous-time bound: $$ \|\mathbb{P}_x(X_t \in \cdot) - \pi(\cdot)\|_f \leqslant \frac{C V(x)}{r_\star(t)} $$

The Fundamental Connection: Point vs. Distributional Convergence

Robbins-Siegmund (Optimization) The noise term is bound by a sequence $c_n$. Because optimization decays the learning rate, the variance sequence is summable ($\sum c_n < \infty$). The energy injections vanish over time, allowing the system to "cool down" entirely and converge almost surely to a singular point mass (the optimum).
Foster-Lyapunov (Markov Chains) The noise term is bound by $b \mathbb{I}_C(\theta)$. In a constant step-size process or an SDE, the noise variance never decays. This indicator function acts as a permanent, non-summable injection of energy every time the process enters the center. The system does not cool to a point, yielding distributional convergence to an invariant measure $\pi$.

Subexponential Lower Bounds (Opposite Condition)

Intuition: When the potential is weakly confining, the restoring force is overpowered by noise. The process wanders, return times become heavy-tailed, and exponential ergodicity breaks.
Submartingale Resistance: To rule out a spectral gap and establish subexponential mixing rates, one must identify $V$ and rate functions $\varphi, \Psi$ where, outside a compact set, $\frac{1}{V(X_t)} - \int_0^t \varphi\left(\frac{1}{V(X_s)}\right)\mathrm{d}s$ is a supermartingale and $\Psi(V(X_t))$ is a submartingale (Brešar & Mijatović, 2024, "Subexponential lower bounds for f-ergodic Markov processes"). This proves the process resists returning to the center.

Part 7

Continuous-Time Limits & Heavy-Tailed Scaling

By scaling the learning rate towards zero, discrete optimization steps coalesce into continuous differential equations. Analyzing the fluctuations around this limit reveals the precise physical nature of the algorithm's noise (whether it diffuses like Gaussian heat or explodes in heavy-tailed jumps).

Finite Variance Limits: LLN and CLT

Intuition: Vanishing step sizes strip away noise, revealing the deterministic mean trajectory (LLN). Scaling the deviations by $1/\sqrt{\eta}$ uncovers the CLT diffusion: Gaussian fluctuations responding to local curvature.
LLN Gradient Flow: As $\eta\to 0$ and $n=\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor\to\infty$, we arrive at the 'LLN limit', which is the gradient flow. More precisely, $$ (\theta_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor})_{t\ge 0}\to \overline{\theta},$$ in the Skorokhod topology (Kushner & Clark, 1978, "Stochastic Approximation Methods for Constrained and Unconstrained Systems"). The macroscopic mean trajectory solves the deterministic ordinary differential equation: $$ \dot{\overline{\theta}}_t = -\nabla L(\overline{\theta}_t) $$
CLT Diffusion Limit: The LLN gradient flow establishes the deterministic path, but completely discards the discrete sampling noise. To model how the optimization process fluctuates around this mean trajectory, we magnify the deviations. Scaling the errors by $1/\sqrt{\eta}$ exposes a Central Limit Theorem (CLT) effect, translating the discrete steps into a continuous diffusion. When the stochastic gradient possesses finite variance, the final iterate converges as $\sqrt{\eta_n^{-1}}(\theta_n - \theta^*) \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$. This asymptotic normal distribution is the stationary distribution of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process driven by standard Brownian motion: $$ \mathrm{d}Z_t = -\mathbb{E}[\nabla^2 l(\theta^*, \xi)]Z_t \mathrm{d}t + \Gamma^{1/2} \mathrm{d}B_t $$ where $\Gamma = \text{Cov}(l(\theta^*, \xi), \nabla l(\theta^*, \xi))$ represents the localized noise covariance.

Bridging SGD and Langevin Dynamics

Langevin Approximation: Cheng, Yin, Bartlett, & Jordan (2020, "Stochastic Gradient and Langevin Processes") establish that the discrete trajectory of SGD can be mapped to a continuous-time diffusion by matching the covariance structure of the gradient noise. By identifying the SGD step as a discrete Euler-Maruyama approximation: $$ \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n - \eta \nabla L(\theta_n) + \sqrt{\eta \Sigma(\theta_n)} \xi_{n+1} $$ If the mini-batch gradient $g(\theta)$ has localized covariance $\Sigma(\theta)$, the corresponding exact Langevin SDE limit is: $$ \mathrm{d}X_t = -\nabla L(X_t) \mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\eta \Sigma(X_t)} \mathrm{d}W_t $$ By bounding the Wasserstein-2 distance between the discrete SGD iterate $\theta_k$ (at time $t = k\eta$) and the continuous diffusion $X_t$, they prove an exact pathwise error bound: $$ W_2(\text{Law}(\theta_k), \text{Law}(X_{k\eta})) \leqslant C \sqrt{\eta} $$ This rigorous mapping proves that SGD strictly inherits the continuous-time mixing properties, stationary distribution, and escaping-time guarantees of the underlying Langevin system, tightly linking discrete optimization rates to diffusion spectral gaps.

Mean-Field Limits & Propagation of Chaos

Intuition: As layer width approaches infinity, parameters become statistically independent (propagation of chaos). Their empirical distribution converges to a macroscopic, deterministic density.
Formal Continuous Limit: Consider a two-layer neural network $h_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma(x; \theta_i)$. As the number of hidden units $N \to \infty$, the discrete empirical measure of the parameters $\hat{\rho}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{\theta_i}$ converges to a continuous probability density $\rho_t(\theta)$.
Propagation of Chaos: In a standard, finite-width neural network, the parameters are intrinsically coupled; the gradient evaluated at $\theta_i$ depends on the positions of all other parameters through the shared empirical loss function. However, as the hidden layer width $N \to \infty$, the empirical sum transitions into a deterministic integral. The individual parameters mathematically decouple from one another—a phenomenon known as propagation of chaos. Instead of directly tracking the complex interactions of millions of individual peers, each parameter interacts solely with the macroscopic probability density $\rho_t$.
McKean-Vlasov SDE & Distributional PDE: This decoupling simplifies the microscopic updates into a macroscopic McKean-Vlasov SDE limit (Mei, Montanari, & Nguyen, 2018, "A mean field view of the landscape of two-layer neural networks"): $$ \mathrm{d}\bar{\theta}_t = -\nabla_\theta \Psi(\bar{\theta}_t; \rho_t) \mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{2T} \mathrm{d}W_t $$ where $\rho_t = \text{Law}(\bar{\theta}_t)$ and $\Psi$ is the functional derivative of the risk. The corresponding non-linear Fokker-Planck equation (continuity equation) governing the density $\rho_t$ is: $$ \partial_t \rho_t(\theta) = \nabla \cdot (\rho_t(\theta) \nabla \Psi(\theta; \rho_t)) + T \Delta \rho_t(\theta) $$
Optimal Transport & Global Convergence: This macroscopic evolution is a Wasserstein gradient flow over the space of probability measures. While the microscopic landscape is highly non-convex in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the macroscopic risk functional is convex with respect to the continuous distribution $\rho$. Under appropriate scaling, this flow converges to the global minimum, explaining why over-parameterized networks reliably avoid suboptimal local minima (Chizat & Bach, 2018, "On the global convergence of gradient descent for over-parameterized models using optimal transport").

The Heavy-Tail Phenomenon & Fractal Geometry

Intuition: Why does Hausdorff dimension link to generalization? Data only determines the loss drift, not the driving noise. Changing the data slightly shifts the loss landscape. The probability of the resulting perturbed path hitting a sharp minimum depends on its spatial volume.
Algorithmic Stability and Hausdorff Dimension: In this approximating SDE framework, the driving Brownian or Lévy noise acts independent of the data; the dataset only dictates the potential function (the loss drift). Consequently, adding or subtracting elements from the dataset shifts the landscape slightly, forcing the parameter path into a perturbed trajectory. The probability that this perturbed path collides with a new, sharp minimum is directly proportional to the spatial "volume" the path occupies. For an $\epsilon$-perturbation, the volume of the tubular neighborhood surrounding the path scales as: $$ V(\epsilon) \approx \epsilon^{d - \dim_H(\mathcal{W})} $$ where $\dim_H(\mathcal{W})$ is the Hausdorff dimension.

The parameter trajectory is modeled as a Feller process $W_t$ taking values in $\mathbb{R}^d$, governed by the SDE: $$ \mathrm{d}W_t = -\nabla f(W_t)\mathrm{d}t + \Sigma_1(W_t)\mathrm{d}B_t + \Sigma_2(W_t)\mathrm{d}L_t^{\alpha(W_t)} $$ where $B_t$ is standard Brownian motion and $L_t^{\alpha(W_t)}$ is an $\alpha$-stable Lévy motion. We analyze the geometric complexity of the continuous path $\mathcal{W} = \{ W_t : t \in [0, T] \}$. The Hausdorff dimension $\dim_H(\mathcal{W})$ measures the geometric thickness or space-filling capacity of the path. It is bounded by the upper Blumenthal-Getoor index $\beta$ of the process. For an $\alpha$-stable Lévy process, $\dim_H(\mathcal{W}) \leqslant \alpha$ almost surely.

Historically, the Hausdorff dimension of the sample paths of Feller processes was established by René Schilling (Schilling, 1998, "Growth and Hölder conditions for the sample paths of Feller processes", Theorem 4), linking the analytic symbol of the differential generator to the path's geometry. Building upon this, Şimşekli, Sagun, & Gürbüzbalaban (2019, "A tail-index analysis of stochastic gradient noise in deep neural networks") demonstrated that the infinite-variance gradient noise found in deep learning shifts the continuous limit of SGD from standard Brownian motion (Hausdorff dimension $2$) to an $\alpha$-stable Lévy process (Hausdorff dimension $\alpha < 2$).

A trajectory with a lower Hausdorff dimension is fundamentally sparser and covers space less densely. When the underlying gradient field shifts slightly due to a data perturbation, a space-filling Brownian path ($V \propto \epsilon^{d-2}$) will likely fall into and get trapped by the shifted sharp minima. In contrast, a low-dimensional Lévy path exhibits long-range jumps that effortlessly leap over these narrow perturbed traps, settling only in wide, flat valleys that are robust to data shifts (Şimşekli, Sener, Deligiannidis, & Erdogdu, 2021, "Hausdorff Dimension, Heavy Tails, and Generalization in Neural Networks"). This provides a geometric link between heavy-tailed noise and model generalization.

Heavy-Tailed Fractional Limits

Fractional Scaling: When the stochastic gradient is regularly varying with index $\alpha \in (1, 2)$ (infinite variance), the scaling must be adjusted. For a constant step size $\eta \to 0$, the scaled trajectory converges in the Skorokhod $J_1$ topology (Blanchet, Mijatović, & Yang, 2024, "Limit theorems for stochastic gradient descent with infinite variance", Theorem 3.1): $$ \eta^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}b_1(\eta^{-1})(\theta_{\lfloor \cdot/\eta \rfloor} - \overline{\theta}(\cdot)) \Rightarrow Z(\cdot) $$ where $b_1$ is a slowly varying function.
The Jump Limit: The limiting stochastic process $Z(\cdot)$ is governed by a time-inhomogeneous SDE: $$ \mathrm{d}Z_t = -\nabla^2 l(\overline{\theta}_t)Z_t \mathrm{d}t + \mathrm{d}L_t $$ Crucially, $L_t$ is an additive process defined by a heavy-tailed jump measure $\nu_t(\mathrm{d}x)$ rather than a Brownian motion, formalizing the emergence of explosive optimization trajectories.

Stationary Distributions & Invariant Measures

Decaying Step-Size Equilibrium: For a decaying step size $\eta_n = c n^{-\rho}$ with $\rho \in (0, 1)$, the scaled parameter error weakly converges to $Z_\infty$, which is the stationary distribution of the Lévy-driven OU process: $$ \mathrm{d}Z_t = -\nabla^2 l(\theta^*)Z_t \mathrm{d}t + \mathrm{d}L_t $$ This yields an explicit analytical solution integrating the heavy-tailed noise against the loss landscape's curvature: $$ Z_\infty = \int_0^\infty \exp(-\nabla^2 l(\theta^*)t) \mathrm{d}L_t $$
Invariant Measure Conditions: For generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, stability and the existence of heavy-tailed invariant measures depend on the jump measure $\nu$ of the driving Lévy process. An invariant probability measure exists if and only if the logarithmic moment condition is satisfied (Behme & Lindner, 2015, "On exponential functionals of Lévy processes"): $$ \int_{\|x\| > 1} \log \|x\| \nu(\mathrm{d}x) < \infty. $$
